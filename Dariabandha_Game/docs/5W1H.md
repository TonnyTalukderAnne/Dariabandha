# 5W1H Framework — Dariabandha: The Guarded Court

## WHO
- **Children** — simple controls (arrows to run, WASD to guard), bright forgiving visuals,
  round-based so losing a round isn't the end of the match.
- **Teenagers / university students** — the reflex/timing skill ceiling and score-chasing
  loop (best-of-N rounds) give it real replay depth against a friend.
- **Families** — one keyboard, two players, sessions as short as 2 minutes or as long as
  a full best-of-9 match; no reading-heavy rules wall before playing.
- **Teachers** — a ready-made, accurate way to introduce a real Bangladeshi folk game and
  its cultural background (docs/RESEARCH.md and the in-game "About" panel) inside a
  classroom setting, alongside the Yutnori project from the same course.
- **International players unfamiliar with Dariabandha** — the interactive tutorial and
  the "why this rule exists" framing in the rules panel explain the *logic* of the game
  (why defenders are confined to lines, why tagging must happen fully in-bounds), not
  just a control scheme to memorize.

## WHAT
Dariabandha is a real-time tag-and-territory duel: a line of guards, each locked to
patrolling one lane, stands between a runner and the far end of the court. The runner
must slip past every guard, touch the far line, and slip past them again on the way
back — untouched — to score. It's built entirely around **spatial reasoning and
timing**, not luck (there's no dice, no randomness in this project — every loss is a
dodge that didn't land). That's the core way it differs from Yutnori, which is a
turn-based race decided largely by throw results. Dariabandha teaches quick
decision-making, pattern-reading (predicting a patrolling guard's rhythm), and
risk-timing (when to commit to a gap).

## WHY
Dariabandha is disappearing from Bangladeshi daily life as open fields shrink and
screens replace them — several of the sources consulted describe it explicitly as
endangered folk heritage, "in the book, not in the field." Rebuilding it as a genuinely
fun browser game is a small, concrete way to keep the mechanic alive for people —
including Bangladeshi kids growing up in cities, and international students who've
never had a reason to encounter it — in the medium they already spend time in. It sits
naturally alongside a Yutnori project as a second, different cultural game from the
same course, letting players compare two very different traditional-game philosophies:
turn-based fortune (Yutnori) vs. real-time skill (Dariabandha).

## WHEN
- Traditionally: the dry season, when harvested fields stood empty and firm underfoot.
- Today: any free moment on a shared computer — a classroom demo, a family game night,
  a study-break duel between roommates, or a cultural festival/booth running it on a
  laptop for passers-by to try in under a minute.

## WHERE
- Traditionally: an open field, lines marked into the earth with a spade.
- Digitally: any modern desktop or laptop browser (this is where the two-keyboard-zone
  hotseat control scheme works best); it also runs on tablets and phones with on-screen
  touch controls, though the fastest, most precise play is on a physical keyboard.
  Runs in any modern desktop, laptop, or tablet browser; deploys cleanly to GitHub
  Pages, Netlify, or Vercel with no build step, or opens directly from a local folder.

## HOW
- **Learn**: a 3-step interactive tutorial (shown once, skippable after) walks through
  what a guard-line is, why the Daria guard can roam, and how scoring works — using the
  actual game board, not a slideshow.
- **Match begins**: both players set nicknames (and optionally upload a photo) and pick
  a match length; a coin-flip animation assigns first roles.
- **Turns/rounds**: each round has one Runner and one Guard (human) plus AI line-guards;
  a round ends the instant the Runner is tagged or completes the full circuit.
- **End of match**: first player to reach the target score wins; a victory screen shows
  the final score and offers rematch or return to menu.
- **Interaction**: keyboard (Arrow keys / WASD) is primary; on-screen touch D-pads
  appear automatically on touch devices.
